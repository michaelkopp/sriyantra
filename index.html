How it is built

Four numbers. The straight figure is Chiodo's: nine triangles whose corners all fall where they must, built from four heights a, b, c, d on the vertical diameter. Everything else follows by ruler and compass — a circle through three points, the meeting of two lines — so the sliders move the construction rather than nudging the drawing towards it.

What is exact about it. The seven places where an apex has to land on another triangle's base, and the twelve triples of lines that have to meet at one point, hold to machine precision at every setting of the four sliders. They are steps of the construction, not conditions being satisfied.

The held conditions. Each switch above the sliders asks for one more thing — the innermost triangle centred on the bindu, or equilateral, or D3's corners on the rim — and solves the remaining freedom for it. Four heights carry at most four conditions, so the switches compete: turn on a fourth and something has to give.

Twelve points, eighteen numbers. The arc figures are built from twelve seed points, and every side is a single circular arc rather than a spline through the nodes — so the curvature of a side is constant by construction, and a straight side is just the zero-curvature case. The eighty-eight incidences hold because each one is a step of the construction.

The grown rings. Outside the nine triangles the figure grows two rings of cells. Ring 1 is built onto its circle here, not fitted to it: the circle is drawn first, each of the ring's fourteen vertices is cut out of it, and the arcs of the yantra are drawn through them. Twelve numbers are free — eleven seeds and the circle's radius — and one more is solved for, to make a single coincidence hold.Ring 2 is built onto its circle in the same way, with eleven free numbers instead of twelve.No ring sits on a circle here, so all eighteen seed numbers are free and nothing is solved for at all. The figure opens on the ring-two shape and lets go from there.

Thirteen corners, twenty-twotwenty numbers. These two are not the nine-triangle figure. The outermost side on each hand is carried out until it closes into a triangle of its own, so there are eleven — six pointing down and five up — and in exchange the fourteen corners the other arc figures hold on the circle are released. Thirteen of the figure’s own corners are placed — nine free in the plane, three on the axis, one on the rim —— seven free in the plane, four on the axis, two on the rim — and the other fifty-twofifty-four objects follow from them.

The ring. What the two new sides cut out of the rim is a ring of tenfourteen cells, and its tenfourteen outer nodes are on the circle exactly. It is not a grown ring and these figures grow none: it is the ring the eleven triangles cut. That is why they wear the lotus and offer no ring switches.

Nothing is arranged. The order these two are replayed from produces every node and every curve from the ones before it, so each step is a circle through points or the crossing of two circles and nothing at all is solved at run time — there is no coincidence to arrange here, and no number that has to be found for one. Move any slider and every incidence holds, the ring’s outer nodes on the rim included. They give out sooner than the other three in exchange: because each corner is determined by the ones before it, a single corner moved on its own runs out of room much earlier than a combination does, which is what the principal directions are for.

Principal directions. The parameters are a fine basis and poor dials: moving one moves the picture by an amount that has nothing to do with moving another. On the built figures the disparity is extreme — sixty steps of "circle through three points" amplify a change in a seed, and the ring's radius moves the innermost triangles four hundred times further than it moves itself. The principal directions are the same space in an order, each scaled so a full slider moves the worst-placed node the same distance as any other.

The bindu. The dot at the centre is the point the innermost triangle is measured against, not an ornament: on the straight figure one of the switches puts it exactly at the centroid.

Note. Every figure here is a construction, and nothing is fitted. On the two ring figures one number is solved for, because one coincidence has to be arranged rather than constructed — the same device Chiodo uses on the straight figure. Everything else, including the whole ring, follows from the order of the steps.

The paper. The straight construction is Chiodo’s: A. Chiodo, On the construction of the Śrī Yantra, C. R. Math. Acad. Sci. Paris 359 (2021), no. 4, 377–397 (doi:10.5802/crmath.163). He proves that the conditions can be met exactly and shows the essential step to be a circle–line–point problem of Apollonius. This viewer and the arc figures are at github.com/michaelkopp/sriyantra.

The paper. The construction is written up in The circular-arc Śrī Yantra, which proves that the classical concurrency conditions hold exactly, in addition to eight further concurrency conditions that hold all the corners of the Śrī Yantra on the circular rim; it counts the family two independent ways and comes with GeoGebra files for the three yantras above. The paper, the GeoGebra files and this viewer are at github.com/michaelkopp/sriyantra.
